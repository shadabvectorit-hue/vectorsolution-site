What does FBR integration cost — and how does the 10% tax credit offset it?

The government side of FBR integration can be free. The engineering side is not — and Finance Act 2026 now gives you 10% of that spend back, if you claim it in the right year.

9 August 20266 min readBy Muhammad Shadab

Ask three people what FBR integration costs and you will hear three numbers, anywhere from zero to several lakh rupees. All three can be honest answers, because the cost has two separate parts: the government side, which is free by law, and the engineering side, which is not. Here is the split as it stands in August 2026, and the new 10% tax credit that offsets a real chunk of it.

The government side is free — by law

FBR is the Federal Board of Revenue, Pakistan's federal tax authority. Its digital invoicing regime runs under SRO 69(I)/2025 — an SRO is a Statutory Regulatory Order, the notification FBR uses to make rules — which rewrote Chapter XIV of the Sales Tax Rules 2006. Under those rules, no fee is payable to FBR itself for integrating. There is no government charge for submitting invoices to the system.

The rules go further. PRAL — Pakistan Revenue Automation Limited, FBR's own technology company — is a licensed integrator by law, and FBR's official FAQ states that PRAL shall "provide free of cost integration services to the registered persons on demand". That includes sandbox testing, the mandatory test environment every business must clear before its production access is switched on.

So the pipe into FBR's system costs nothing: your API credentials, the technical documentation, the sandbox, and production access all come free through PRAL.

Why your total cost still is not zero

Here is the part sales pitches skip. As integration vendors note, PRAL's free route provides the connection — not a product. You get access to FBR's system; you do not get invoicing software or a user interface. Something on your side still has to raise the invoice, and that something must:

  • build every invoice in the exact data format FBR's validator accepts;
  • carry the correct HS code — Harmonized System code, the international commodity classification FBR checks on every line item — and a matching unit of measure;
  • clear the sandbox test scenarios assigned to your business type;
  • print the FBR invoice number and QR code on every invoice it issues.

That is the engineering bill: either your existing ERP or POS taught to do all of this, or software that has it built in. The honest arithmetic is simple — Rs 0 on the government side, plus a real one-time project on the software side.

What commercial licensed integrators charge

PRAL is not the only door. FBR licenses commercial integrators too, and the official list on FBR's website has grown through 2025 and 2026 — check the live page rather than trusting names in old blog posts. These firms are allowed to charge for integration, but not freely: under the rules their fees are subject to caps the Board specifies.

What does the market actually look like? Industry comparison guides put typical e-invoicing packages at roughly Rs 5–20 per invoice, while enterprise rate cards run far higher — one flagship integrator's published schedule listed Rs 1,500,000 in one-time setup plus annual maintenance of Rs 60 per invoice or Rs 3,500,000 a year. Pricing is genuinely all over the map, which is why a written quote matters more than any figure in an article, including this one.

One useful protection: STGO 01 of 2026 — an STGO is a Sales Tax General Order, FBR's operational instruction — expressly allows a registered person to engage one or more licensed integrators. You are not locked in. Choosing between the free PRAL route and a commercial integrator is its own decision, and we cover it in the next article: PRAL or an integrator?

Section 64D: the 10% tax credit, rule by rule

Finance Act 2026, effective 1 July 2026, inserted Section 64D into the Income Tax Ordinance 2001. It expands what used to be a narrow credit limited to POS machines (capped at Rs 150,000 per machine) into a broad offset against integration spending, and its rules as reported are precise:

  • 10% of eligible investment. The credit equals 10% of the amount spent on the purchase, acquisition, installation and implementation of equipment, hardware, software and other electronic components used to integrate with FBR's computerised system.
  • Broad eligibility. It covers any person required to integrate under the Income Tax Ordinance 2001, the Sales Tax Act 1990, or the Federal Excise Act 2005 — not just retailers.
  • Normal tax only. The credit adjusts only against normal tax liability. If your business pays mostly minimum or final tax, it may be worth little to you — ask your consultant before counting on it.
  • Running costs excluded. Operational, maintenance and recurring expenses after installation do not qualify. Annual support fees and subscriptions likely sit outside the credit.
  • No cap notified — yet. As of August 2026 no monetary ceiling has been notified, but FBR retains the authority to prescribe further conditions and restrictions, so this can change.

The timing rule is the trap. The credit is claimable only in the tax year in which the electronic resources are installed, integrated and fully configured with FBR's system. One year, one shot — no carry-forward has been reported. If your project straddles a year-end, the date configuration completes decides which year the claim belongs to. Keep every invoice, itemised so that hardware, software and implementation are separate from support charges, and let your tax consultant make the claim.
