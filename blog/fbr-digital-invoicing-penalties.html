What does not integrating with FBR digital invoicing actually cost?

The real penalty ladder from the Sales Tax Act — Rs 500,000 to Rs 3,000,000 and premises sealing — plus the buyer-side risks Finance Act 2026 added, and the one waiver that rewards moving early.

9 August 20267 min readBy Muhammad Shadab

Every sales-tax-registered business in Pakistan was supposed to be issuing invoices through FBR's digital invoicing system by 31 December 2025, when the last phase of the onboarding schedule under SRO 1852(I)/2025 closed. (FBR is the Federal Board of Revenue; an SRO, a Statutory Regulatory Order, is the notification it uses to make rules.) No blanket extension followed in 2026. The live question now is what staying out actually costs.

The answer sits in the penalty table of section 33 of the Sales Tax Act 1990, at serial number 25A. It is steeper and more mechanical than most of the figures circulating online — and, in one specific way, more forgiving than you might expect.

What section 33, serial 25A actually says

Serial 25A applies to any person required to integrate under section 23 of the Act who fails to do so, and equally to a person who integrates but then fails to issue electronic invoices. The penalties escalate by default, and each next rung lands if you are still non-compliant fifteen days after the previous penalty order:

DefaultPenaltyWhen it lands
FirstRs 500,000Failure to integrate, or to issue electronic invoices after integrating
SecondRs 1,000,000Still in default fifteen days after the first-default order
ThirdRs 2,000,000Still in default fifteen days after the second-default order
FourthRs 3,000,000Still in default fifteen days after the third-default order

Note what this is not. It is not a per-invoice fine and not a daily fine. It is a ladder of default orders, and the fifteen-day spacing means a business that ignores its first order can, on paper, be Rs 6,500,000 deep within roughly seven weeks. The money is also not the end of it.

Premises can be sealed at any rung. Serial 25A adds that the business premises of a non-compliant person "shall be liable to be sealed by an officer of Inland Revenue" — and the Act applies this alongside the monetary penalties, not as a final step after the fourth default.

Now the forgiving part. A proviso to serial 25A lets the Commissioner waive the first-default penalty of Rs 500,000 entirely if the business integrates before the second-default penalty is imposed. The proviso is worded for retailers, so have your tax consultant confirm how it applies to your case — but the design intent is plain: the law rewards the business that moves after the first notice, and escalates hard against the one that does not.

One neighbouring row is worth knowing too. Serial 24 catches a business that has integrated but issues invoices without the prescribed FBR invoice number, barcode or QR code, or with duplicate or counterfeit ones. That costs Rs 500,000 or 200% of the tax involved, whichever is higher, with prosecution possible on conviction. Integrating and then quietly bypassing the system is not a loophole; it is a separate offence.

The figures you can ignore — and the row people confuse

Search for FBR e-invoicing penalties and you will find confident claims of Rs 50,000 or 2% of tax per invoice, or Rs 25,000 per day. Neither figure appears in the section 33 table of the consolidated Sales Tax Act, and at least one industry compliance guide has warned that they are not in the statute. What does exist is the residual penalty at serial 19 — Rs 5,000 or 3% of the tax involved, whichever is higher — for contraventions with no specific entry. Real, but not the e-invoicing ladder.

The other common mix-up is serial 25, one row up. That entry covers integration for production monitoring, tracking and tracing under section 40C — factory-side monitoring, a different obligation entirely. After Finance Act 2026 it carries a penalty of up to Rs 1,000,000, a further penalty of up to Rs 5,000,000 if the offence continues a month later, and sealing. If a blog quotes Rs 5,000,000 for digital invoicing, it has read the wrong row. For not integrating with digital invoicing, the operative ladder is serial 25A: Rs 500,000 to Rs 3,000,000, plus sealing.

Finance Act 2026 made this bigger than fines

From 1 July 2026, Finance Act 2026 widened section 21 of the Sales Tax Act: FBR may now de-register, blacklist, or suspend the registration of a business that fails to integrate its e-invoicing system. Suspension is quietly the harshest item on this page — a business whose sales tax registration is suspended cannot issue valid tax invoices at all.

The same Act also moved pressure to the other side of the invoice. Three buyer-side mechanics matter as of August 2026:

  • Input tax on non-electronic invoices is at risk. A buyer must hold a valid tax invoice to claim input tax, and a paper invoice from a supplier who is legally required to issue electronic invoices may not qualify as one — putting the buyer's input tax claim at risk. No single clause says this outright, but the statutory chain points that way.
  • A 20% penalty on unmatched input tax. Where FBR's system finds input tax that cannot be matched to output tax declared by the supplier, the claimant pays a penalty of 20% of the unmatched amount, on top of reversing the credit plus default surcharge, after notice and a hearing.
  • A public register of simulated invoice issuers. A person issuing fictitious invoices pays a penalty equal to their face value and is listed publicly. Input tax claimed on a listed issuer's invoices is reversed automatically, and a buyer who fails to reverse it within sixty days pays a further 20%.

The commercial consequence is simple: registered buyers now have every reason to verify that their suppliers are integrated before they claim input tax. A non-integrated supplier is not just carrying its own penalty risk — it is handing risk to its customers, and customers walk away from that.

Where enforcement stands, as of August 2026

Here is what can honestly be said. Non-integration notices were reported from late 2025, and industry guides report enforcement from January 2026 — that timing comes from vendor compliance guides rather than official FBR announcements, so treat it as reported, not gazetted. The legal machinery above, however, is fully in force, and the sealing power is not theoretical: in September 2025, FBR officials in Islamabad sealed Saeed Book Bank, one of Asia's largest bookstores, for failing to integrate its point-of-sale system, then de-sealed it about two days later once management committed to comply.

That case is the pattern in miniature. Enforcement arrives suddenly, it is public, and it ends when you integrate. Which points to the obvious conclusion.

The cheapest exit is the boring one

Add it up. A first default costs Rs 500,000. A professional integration costs a fraction of that and, in our projects, a standard ERP or POS integration runs 2–4 weeks including sandbox testing. The law even helps the early mover: the first-default waiver above, plus rule 150V of the Sales Tax Rules, under which the Commissioner can extend an integration deadline by up to 60 days in 15-day steps, with paper invoices permitted during the extension. And there is a carrot — Section 64D of the Income Tax Ordinance gives a 10% tax credit on qualifying integration spend, claimable in the year your system is installed and fully configured. Integrating after the second default costs the same project plus Rs 1,500,000 in penalties, with your premises exposed in between.
